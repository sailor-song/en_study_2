Should we make New Year's resolutions?

As the clock strikes midnight on December 31st, millions of people worldwide embark on a tradition as old as the Roman Empire: making New Year's resolutions. Whether it's a commitment to fitness, learning a new language, or saving money, the psychological impulse to start anew is powerful.

However, research suggests that approximately 80% of resolutions fail by the second week of February. This phenomenon leads many to question whether the practice is inherently flawed or if our approach to goal-setting requires a fundamental shift.

The concept of the "Fresh Start Effect" explains why we feel more motivated at the beginning of cycles—be it a new year, a new month, or even a Monday. These temporal landmarks allow us to distance ourselves from our past mistakes and view our future selves with renewed optimism.

"The secret of change is to focus all of your energy, not on fighting the old, but on building the new."

To make resolutions stick, experts recommend "atomic habits"—breaking down large ambitions into tiny, manageable actions. Instead of "mastering English," one might resolve to "read one article on Obsidian English every morning." The specificity reduces cognitive load and makes consistency more achievable.
